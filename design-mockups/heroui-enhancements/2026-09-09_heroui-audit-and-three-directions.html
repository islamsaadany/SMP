Design read-across · 9 September 2026

You do not need HeroUI. You need its vocabulary.

HeroUI cannot be installed in SMP — but reading its catalogue against ours answers a question nobody has asked out loud: which parts of a component library have we already built one class at a time, and which are genuinely missing?

Subject heroui-inc/heroui · React 19 + Tailwind v4 + React Aria Measured against SMP v3.22, Mobile · Performance Status proposal — nothing built
01

The library will not fit, and it is worth saying why first

HeroUI is a React component library. It needs React 19, Tailwind v4 and a build step, and it ships as npm packages — @heroui/button, @heroui/modal — that a bundler tree-shakes into an application.

SMP is one self-contained HTML file. No React, no Tailwind, no bundler, no npm — package.json deliberately has no build script. It is assembled by concatenating sources with Python, it runs offline from a memory stick, and since the security pass its script blocks are locked to their exact bytes. Every one of those is a decision we made on purpose, and adopting HeroUI would reverse all of them at once.

So this is a read-across, not an adoption. Three things travel without the code: a checklist of what a mature component library carries, three primitives we are genuinely missing, and a naming vocabulary that would tidy something the numbers below say is worth tidying.

02

What our own sources say

Counted this morning across the nine stylesheets, before anything was proposed:

57
distinct class names ending in btn, cta, chip or pill
19
of those are chips and pills alone — nineteen ways to draw one small rounded label
116
focus rules across ten files, and no check anywhere measures a single one
3
files that hand-write the same “this box scrolls” fade
9,545
lines of CSS carrying all of it

Every one of those class names was the right call on the day it was written. The cost is not any single one — it is that a new control has fifty-seven precedents to choose between, and the person choosing is usually me at two in the morning.

03

Their catalogue against ours

HeroUI ships about seventy-five components. Here is every one that touches something SMP actually does — and the honest verdict is that we have most of them already. That is the finding, not a disappointment.

They shipWhat we have todayVerdict
Meter · Progress One gauge, on the company page. Everywhere else a figure is read against a benchmark as text — 48% / 90%. Take it The only real reading gain on the list. Direction A below.
ScrollShadow Written by hand three times — the Setup rail, the settings panel, the plan tables. Take it One primitive replacing three. Invisible to you; three fewer places to get it wrong.
Button (variant × tone × size) 57 class names, no shared vocabulary. Two of them have already collided by accident. Take the naming Not the code. One button, a few axes. Nothing on screen moves.
Kbd Nothing. The presentation's arrows, Escape and page keys are undiscoverable — we removed the on-screen bar on purpose. Take it Small: one key-hints card behind “?” while presenting.

05

Three directions

Each is drawn on the same screen so they can be compared. Each is a different bet about what is wrong. Only one of them should be built.

A · Pace

Recommended

The bet: the numbers are right and shapeless. Nothing is added to the data and nothing is recalculated — the figures already on the card gain a shape, so a row is judged by looking rather than by reading. This is HeroUI's Meter, and nothing else from it.

What it buys

The spread on a card stops being two numbers you subtract in your head. Four rows in a column become one shape you run your eye down. Nothing is recalculated — the bar on a card is the Lowest and Highest already printed under it.

What it costs

One new primitive to maintain, and 40px of card height — measured, the table starts 367px down instead of 327px. The Progress column widens from 100 to 150px, which the measures table has spare and the tactics table does not — so the bar goes on measures and objectives only, and I would say so rather than squeeze it in.

The part to watch

The projector must learn it too. A slide that prints the number while the page shows a bar means the room and the laptop disagree, which is the one thing a review cannot afford.

B · Flat tint

HeroUI's own look

The bet: state should be carried by the surface, not by a label in the corner. This is what HeroUI actually looks like — soft tinted grounds, larger radii, no dark furniture. The card is the status; the pill becomes a caption rather than the message.

What it buys

The page reads softer and more current, and a card's state is legible from further away than a pill is — useful on a projector at the back of a room.

What it reverses

The navy table head is not decoration: it is the one surface that wears the client's brand colour, so a light head means a tenant's colours survive only in the top bar. We settled that once already, in the other direction.

What it spends

Three tinted cards in a row spend most of the colour budget the scoring bands live on. When everything is tinted, a tint stops meaning “look here” — and the amber card and the amber Fill in what is empty button start arguing.

C · Density

HeroUI's size axis

The bet: one size does not serve both jobs. The office scans tables on a laptop all day; a unit head presents the same page to a room. HeroUI carries a size on every component; here it becomes one switch per person — Compact or Comfortable — and Compact is what you would work in.

What it buys

Measured on these two drawings: the table starts 202px down where today it starts at 327, and a row is 26px where today it is 35. In the same height that holds one table today, Compact holds the measures and the tactics — which is the pair you actually read together.

What it costs

A second layout to test on every page, on both sides of the units-and-functions switch. Every table-fits-its-pane measurement doubles. That is real work, and it is the honest reason to build it last rather than first.

The part to watch

Compact is wrong on a projector. If it is remembered per person and somebody presents from their own laptop, the room gets the dense view — so it would have to stand itself down while presenting, which is one more rule.

What I would do, and what I need from you

Take A, and take the three invisible ones beside it. A is the only direction that makes a number easier to read without reopening a decision we have already argued once. The scroll fade, the button naming and the key hints change nothing on screen and cost a day between them.

B is a real look and the wrong trade. It is what HeroUI is for, and it spends the two things this product is short of — the client's brand colour on the table head, and the colour budget the bands need. If you want the softer look, the honest version is to take it on the cards only and leave the tables alone.

C is right and not yet. Every complaint about a squeezed column is really this, and it is the largest of the three to test properly. Worth doing after a reporting round, not during one.

  1. Which direction — A, B, C, or a piece of one? Nothing is built either way.
  2. If A: does the bar go on the tactics table too? I have left it off because that table is already the widest thing in the product, and I would rather say so than squeeze it.
  3. The three invisible ones — scroll fade, button naming, key hints while presenting. May I take those regardless of which direction you pick?
