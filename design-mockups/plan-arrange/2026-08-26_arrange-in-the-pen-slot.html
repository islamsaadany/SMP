Arrange, back in the plan

"I will give it back — shall we align where to visually have it? I was thinking it can land in the same place of the pen for the non-SMO roles, and it can be another shape that indicates arrangement, and it's only in the strategy plan part which mirrors in the reporting and performance."

Where we are

The slot is already there, and for most people it is empty

A unit's Plan pane carries one control in its top-right corner. The office gets a pen; a unit head or strategy custodian gets nothing at all. Every image on this page is the real platform — same build, same data, the candidates injected into the live pane rather than drawn from the stylesheet.

Three ways to fill it

What goes in the corner

They are mutually exclusive by role — whoever has the pen never sees the arrange control, and whoever sees it never has a pen — so this is one slot with two occupants, not two controls competing for a corner.

A

The grip mark

ForIt wears the same mark as the thing it turns on. The drag handles already in every row are three bars; a button that reveals them and is drawn as one of them needs no explaining. Same 28px circle, same corner, same gold fill when live — it reads as the pen's sibling because structurally it is.

AgainstThree bars is also the universal “menu” glyph. Mitigated by the corner it sits in and by nothing else on the pane opening a menu, but it is a real ambiguity and worth naming.

B

The up-down arrows

ForUnambiguous: nobody reads a vertical double arrow as a menu. It says reorder on its own, without needing the handles to have been seen first.

AgainstIt is a generic icon borrowed from outside the product, and it matches nothing else on the page. The handles it turns on look nothing like it, so pressing it produces a mark the button did not predict.

C

The word, moved into the corner

ForIt is the shape the group's own Arrange button already has, so the two halves of the product would say the same thing the same way. No icon to interpret at all.

AgainstIt breaks the corner. A 28px circle becomes a 74px pill that shifts to “Done” mid-use, and the pane's title row was measured for a round button — the band has 34px of clearance, not 80.

The recommendation

A, and the reason is the handles

The only argument that decides this is that the button should look like what it produces. Press it and three-bar grips appear down the left of every row. B promises reordering and delivers a mark it never showed you; A promises the exact mark you are about to get.

The menu-glyph ambiguity is real and I would take it: nothing on this pane opens a menu, the control is a lone circle in a corner rather than an item in a bar, and it carries a tooltip and an aria-label reading Arrange. If it turns out to confuse people, B is a one-line change — the slot, the states and the wiring are identical.

What the change actually is

Five constraints, four of them not visual

Where it appears
The strategy Plan pane only, exactly as you said. Performance and Reporting need nothing: the order is the array, so both already follow whatever the Plan says.
Who sees it
Anybody who may arrange but has no pen — unit head, strategy custodian, supporting function head. The office keeps the pen, which already turns the handles on.
The server has to change
This is the part that is not a button. lib/authorize.js compares row ids in order and classifies a reordering as authoring, so it is refused on save today. Without that change they would drag, and the save would fail silently.
What it reverses
§94.15 removed this button, and its stated reason was that everyone who could arrange already had a pen. Giving arrangement back to people with no pen makes that sentence untrue, so the button gets its audience back. Recorded as a reversal, not a re-invention.
What proves it
Both ends, per §94.2: the control is present and pressable for a unit head, absent for the office (who have the pen), and a drag performed as a unit head is accepted by the authoriser — the assertion that would have caught the silent refusal.

What I need from you

  1. A, B or C — I recommend A.
  2. Does a contributor get it? They can see the plan but not author it. I would say no: arranging is a decision about the plan, and a contributor reports against it.
  3. Does it reach a supporting function's Projects pane too, or units only? The same pane and the same slot exist there.
