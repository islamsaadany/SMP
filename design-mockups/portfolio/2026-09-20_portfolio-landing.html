What this screen is

One question, and the row answers it four ways. Which project needs me — how far along it is, whether it is late, what is waiting on somebody, and when anybody next looks at it. Nothing else is on it. The charter, the tree, the queue and the client-facing reading are four tabs one press away, and repeating any of them here would be the same fault §9.12 kept off Analytics.

A project has no status field

§5 stores a name, a charter and a checkpoint cadence — and nothing that says how the project is going. Every figure in this list is worked out from the plan underneath it, which is §9.8's rule one level up and is what makes this list unable to disagree with the pages behind it. Culture Transformation's 59%, its Behind mark and its 5 are the same rows and the same sums the Plan, Progress and Analytics read.

Two of these have no figure, and that is the honest answer

  • Retail network review has a charter and no plan. There is nothing to average, so the row says so rather than printing 0% — absent is not nought (§35, §93). A project is drafted and worked on and nothing gates building its plan (§5.1), so this is a normal state rather than a broken one.
  • Finance systems consolidation has a plan nobody has started. That is nought, and it reads 0% — which is the difference the row above it exists to show.

Late and how far along are two questions

The word beside the bar answers how far along — Not started, Early, Under way, Nearly done, Done — and the red mark answers is it late. They are drawn apart for the reason §9.12 gives: On Track is the reference's word for 90% and is a claim about a schedule the figure cannot see (§344). A project can be 59% and behind, and both are true.

What is waiting is a count of rows, never of reasons

Three things wait on somebody — work marked done and not signed off, work past its date, and work nobody is on. An activity that is two of those is owed once, so the number is the union by row and never the three lengths added up (§279, §108.1). The breakdown underneath says which, and pressing it opens Progress, where they are three sections.

Commercial excellence programme is drawn to show it rather than leave it as a claim (§255): its promotion review is past its date and has nobody on it, so the breakdown names three reasons and the number reads 2. A list that added the reasons up would say 3 and send somebody looking for a row that is not there.

The list is already narrowed, and the strip counts what you can open

A seat on the client reaches every project; anybody else reaches only the ones that name them (§6.1). Press Omar Tarek — he is on two — and the figures at the top move with the list, because a headline counting projects somebody cannot open would be telling them about work they have no way in to (§108.1). Nothing is hidden from anybody who can see a project at all: what changes is which projects are yours, never how much of one you are shown.

Starting a project is by seat (§5, decision 5), so Omar is offered no button rather than a dead one (§61, §301).

The gold edge means this one is yours to do (§9.10's own rule) and is drawn for Omar and not for the seat: everything is a seat's, so marking all five would be a colour that means nothing (§41's budget).

Somebody with one project never sees this page

§4 row 5: a client with one project lands in it. So this list is only ever drawn for somebody who can reach two or more, which is why there is no one-row state to look at. Omar is drawn on two for that reason.

The order is the plan's own start, earliest first

Not worst first. A short list is scanned for a name, and an order that moves as things go wrong is one you cannot scan — the marks and the strip do the alerting instead. It is the same argument §9.12 made for putting the commitments in date order rather than by severity. A project with no plan has no start date and sorts last.

A table rather than cards

Cards are this platform's shape for picking a destination, and they are the wrong shape here: the whole job of this screen is comparing four facts down four columns, which a card grid breaks up. The cost is that a project's brief is one line rather than a paragraph, and it fits the pane at 1180 and below without scrolling sideways (§158).

What it leaves open

  • Does a finished project stay in this list for ever? Supply chain resilience is drawn in it, because nothing in §5 retires a project and a client with twelve years of work would eventually scroll. An archive, a fold, or nothing at all — not decided, and a decision rather than a detail.
  • Nothing here is printable. Same as Analytics (§9.12), and the same answer: Strategy's own routes are what it would follow.

And it was rendered before publishing

The three drawings before this one say in their own notes that they were not — those containers had no browser. This one does, so every figure on this page was read out of the rendered document rather than checked by eye, and the Plan, Progress and Analytics drawings were rendered beside it and proved byte-identical to what they published (§303).
