What this tab is, and what it is not

  • It is the reading you take into a review. Where each part of the plan stands, and every commitment with whether it was met. That is all it holds.
  • It is not the working queue. What is late, what is waiting for a sign-off and what has nobody on it live on Progress, because those are jobs rather than a reading. Nothing is repeated across the two.
  • And it is not the plan. The activity-by-activity detail and the timeline are the Plan tab. This is the level above them.
  • Everything here is derived from the same rows those two pages read. Nothing is typed and nothing is stored — so this page cannot say something the plan does not.

The two numbers, and how they are worked out

  • Progress rolls up one level at a time. An activity's figure comes from its sub-activities by weight; a phase's from its activities; the project's from its phases. Equal weights unless somebody sets them, which is this platform's own rule at every level above an activity.
  • A milestone is hit when it was signed off on or before the day it was due — and not otherwise. Their version counts a milestone delivered three weeks late as hit, which makes the headline figure say the opposite of what happened.
  • Marked done is not hit. A commitment whose work is finished and waiting for a Lead to sign it off is drawn in the platform's own navy and counted in neither column. Their version counts it as delivered on time, because an activity with no recorded end date is assumed to have met its date — which flatters exactly the backlog it sits beside.
  • Which is why phase 2 reads 2 of 3 done while its commitment still reads Awaiting sign-off. That is not a contradiction and it is the point: progress counts work that has been finished, and a commitment is met when somebody accepts it. The blueprint draft is finished. Nobody has accepted it yet.
  • How far along is not the same as on schedule, so the page says both. A phase can be nearly done and still behind, and the word beside the bar answers the first question while the red mark answers the second. Their label for a phase at 90% is On Track, which is a claim about the date made by a figure that cannot see it.

The commitments are in date order, on purpose

  • A review walks the timeline. Theirs sorts by severity — overdue first, then upcoming, then what was met — which is the right order for somebody triaging and the wrong one for somebody reading a project out. The outstanding ones stand out by colour without having to be dragged to the top.
  • Six milestones on nine activities is not a real plan. This one has been given a commitment in every state the tracker can draw so all six are visible at once. A real project has far fewer.

Two things left out, and one still open

  • No health score. Theirs is a single 0–100 whose weights do not survive being looked at: an empty plan and an untouched plan both score 50, a completely overdue plan cannot go below 20, and four blocked activities cost a fifth of the score whether the plan has four or four hundred. If Portfolio ever wants one number it should decide its own rather than inherit that.
  • No phase groups. Coloured buckets a Lead drags phases into, to group this page by. Left out of the first build: their own page synthesises every ungrouped phase into a group of one, so dropping it turns this section into the plain phase list above and changes nothing else — and their version is 642 lines, of which about 90 are the dragging.
  • Still open: across projects. Their page is per client and aggregates every plan at once. This one is per project, and the client-wide reading belongs on the landing — the project list, with each project's state. That is not drawn yet.
